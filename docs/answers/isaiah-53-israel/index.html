Answer an objection

Isaiah 53 is about the people of Israel, not Jesus.

30-second answer

The servant of Isaiah 53 is Jesus. Read the chapter closely and the servant is one person, not the nation. In Isaiah everyone confesses sin, the prophet included, but the servant alone is sinless. He is punished 'for the transgression of My people' (BSB), so he is someone other than the people. He is the one true Israel who brings Israel back to God. He gives his life as a guilt offering for others, dies and is buried with a rich man, and then lives again and sees his offspring. Only Jesus fits every line, and the New Testament says so plainly. Many early Jewish sources, such as the Targum and the Talmud, connect the passage with the Messiah. The 'servant is Israel' reading is old too (Origen met it around AD 248), but it became the majority Jewish view only after Rashi in the Middle Ages.

Evidence

  • Only the servant is sinless. In Isaiah everyone confesses sin, the prophet too: 'Woe is me, for I am ruined, because I am a man of unclean lips' (Isaiah 6:5), and 'We all like sheep have gone astray' and the LORD laid on the servant 'the iniquity of us all' (Isaiah 53:6). But of the servant alone it says 'He had done no violence, nor was any deceit in His mouth' (Isaiah 53:9, BSB).
  • He suffers for the people, so he is not the people. Isaiah 53:8 says 'He was stricken for the transgression of My people' (BSB). The prophet's people are Israel, and the servant is punished on their behalf.
  • He is the one true Israel, who restores Israel. Isaiah 49:3 does call this servant 'Israel'. Yet two verses later the LORD formed him 'to bring Jacob back to Him' and 'to raise up the tribes of Jacob, and to restore the protected ones of Israel' (Isaiah 49:5-6, BSB). Christians answer that the servant is the faithful Israelite who embodies the nation and brings it back, so he is an individual distinct from the nation he restores.
  • He takes our place willingly. 'the LORD has laid upon Him the iniquity of us all' (Isaiah 53:6), 'He did not open His mouth' (53:7), and 'His soul is made a guilt offering' (53:10). This is one innocent person carrying the guilt of many, as a sacrifice.
  • He dies and then lives. He is 'cut off from the land of the living' and given 'a grave with the wicked, and with a rich man in His death' (53:8-9), yet afterward 'He will see His offspring, He will prolong His days' and 'will see the light of life' (53:10-11). That is death followed by resurrection. Jesus was crucified between criminals, and then 'there came a rich man from Arimathea named Joseph' who 'placed it in his own new tomb that he had cut into the rock' (Matthew 27:57-60, BSB). On the third day he rose.
  • The New Testament names him. Philip read this very passage to the Ethiopian official 'and told him the good news about Jesus' (Acts 8:32-35). Peter quotes it of Christ: 'He committed no sin, and no deceit was found in His mouth' and 'By His stripes you are healed' (1 Peter 2:22-25). Jesus himself said, “this Scripture must be fulfilled in Me: ‘And He was numbered with the transgressors.’” (Luke 22:37).
  • The earliest church read it the same way. Clement of Rome (about AD 96) quotes Isaiah 53 at length as spoken of 'Our Lord Jesus Christ' (1 Clement 16), and Justin Martyr (about AD 155) quotes Isaiah 52:10 to 54:6 to show that sins are forgiven through Christ's death (Dialogue with Trypho, chapter 13).
  • Important early Jewish sources connect the servant with the Messiah. The Aramaic paraphrase of Isaiah (Targum Jonathan) opens the passage with 'Behold my servant Messiah shall prosper' (Isaiah 52:13), though it applies the sufferings of chapter 53 mostly to others: the nation, the Temple and the wicked. The Talmud (Sanhedrin 98b) calls the Messiah 'the leprous one' or 'the sick one', citing Isaiah 53:4. Much later, the Zohar (about the 13th century) pictures the Messiah taking Israel's sicknesses and pains on himself, quoting "He was wounded for our transgressions" (Driver and Neubauer's 1877 translation, vol. 2, pp. 14-15). These are collected in English in Driver and Neubauer's 1876-77 volumes (see links).
  • Some medieval Jewish teachers saw the problem with the Israel reading. Moshe Kohen ibn Crispin, a 14th-century commentator in Spain, wrote that reading the servant as the nation 'distorts the passage from its natural sense', because the chapter speaks of one person throughout, and he chose to explain it 'in accordance with the teaching of our Rabbis, of the King Messiah' (Driver and Neubauer, vol. 2, pp. 99-100). He did not accept Jesus, but he saw that the text describes one person, the Messiah.
  • The Israel reading is old too. Origen (about AD 248) tells of a debate in which a Jewish teacher said these prophecies 'bore reference to the whole people, regarded as one individual' (Contra Celsum 1.55). Origen answered that the one who 'bears our sins' must be someone other than the sinners he heals.
  • Jewish sources here are paraphrased or quoted only from the public-domain 1877 English translation; read them for yourself at the links.

Still debated

The 'servant is Israel' reading is real and has a long history, and Isaiah calls Israel God's servant in other chapters (41:8; 49:3). It was known as early as the third century (Origen, Against Celsus 1.55, reports a Jewish teacher reading it of the dispersed nation), but it became the majority Jewish reading only after Rashi, in the medieval period. Early sources such as the Targum and the Talmud, and later the Zohar, connect the passage with the Messiah, and the details of the chapter point to Jesus.

Where this pointsIsaiah 53 is fulfilled in Jesus: He died for our sins according to the Scriptures, was buried, and was raised on the third day (1 Corinthians 15:3-8). See the evidence for the resurrection →
