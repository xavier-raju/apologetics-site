Learn

Short lessons, then a quick quiz to see what stuck.

Lessons

Historians judge ancient people by sources: how many, how early, how independent. Jesus has Paul's letters (50s), four Gospels (about AD 65 to 100, and many date some before 70), and non-Christian writers like Josephus, Tacitus, and Pliny.

That is a lot for a traveling teacher from a small province. It's why almost every historian, believing or not, agrees he lived and was crucified under Pilate.

Takeaway: Start with what nearly everyone agrees on. That's solid ground.

  • Josephus, a Jewish historian (about AD 93), mentions James, "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ" (Antiquities 20.9.1).
  • Tacitus, a Roman historian (about AD 116), says Christ was executed by Pontius Pilate (Annals 15.44).
  • Pliny the Younger, a Roman governor (about AD 112), says Christians met before dawn and recited "a form of prayer to Christ, as to a divinity": Letters 10.96 (X.97 in the Melmoth/Bosanquet numbering).

These writers weren't fans. That's what makes them useful.

Honest note: a longer Josephus passage was probably touched up by later Christian copyists. Most scholars think a shorter original mentioned Jesus.

We don't have the originals of any ancient book. We have copies. For the New Testament we have about 5,500 to 5,700 Greek manuscripts, more than any other ancient work.

Copies differ. Scholar Peter Gurry estimates about 500,000 variants, not counting spelling. That sounds scary until you see why: more copies means more chances to count differences. Most are spelling, word order, or slips.

Some passages were added later, and we say so: John 5:4, Mark 16:9-20, and John 7:53-8:11 are not in the earliest copies. Most Bibles mark them. No core belief depends on them.

Takeaway: Because we have so many copies, scholars can spot changes. That's a strength.

Jesus died by crucifixion. His followers believed they saw him alive. Paul the persecutor changed. James the skeptic changed. The tomb was empty.

Gary Habermas argues most scholars accept these facts (his survey, his claim). The question is what best explains them. Theft, fainting, wrong tomb, and hallucinations each leave facts unexplained. We think "He is risen" explains them best.

1 Peter 3:15 asks us to give a reason for our hope, and to "respond with gentleness and respect" (BSB).

  • Listen first. Ask, "What makes you think that?"
  • Say what's true in their point.
  • Give one clear answer, not ten.
  • Admit what's still debated. Honesty builds trust.
  • Never mock. Pray for them.

Muslims honor Jesus as a prophet. Jewish friends read their Scriptures through a long, rich tradition. Latter-day Saints and Jehovah's Witnesses love Jesus but describe him differently than historic Christianity.

Learn their view in their words before responding. Start from what you share, then gently point to Jesus' death, resurrection, and identity. See "What others say" for each.
