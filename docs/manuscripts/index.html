Manuscripts

How we know the text wasn't changed, using numbers that hold up.

Can we trust that the Bible we read is what they wrote?

None of the original pages written by Matthew, Mark, Luke, John or Paul survive. That is true of almost every book from the ancient world. What we have are copies, and copies of copies.

That sounds like bad news, but it is where the good news starts. The New Testament was copied more often than any other book from the ancient world. About 5,500 to 5,700 handwritten Greek copies survive, from small scraps to complete Bibles. Some fragments are probably from the 2nd century, and two whole Bibles survive from the 4th century.

With that many copies from so many places, scholars can compare them and see where a scribe slipped. When one copy goes wrong, the others show it. So the large number of differences between copies is a sign of how much we have to work with, not of a text that was lost.

On these pages we give you the real numbers, the oldest copies you can look at online, and an honest look at the few places where later copies added something. We also list some popular numbers we do not use, because the case is strong enough without them.

Key facts

about 5,500 to 5,700
About 5,500 to 5,700 handwritten Greek copies of all or part of the New Testament survive.

The official register (the Kurzgefasste Liste in Muenster) was reported at 5,711 in April 2026 by the Evangelical Textual Criticism blog (Peter Gurry, ETC blog, 2 April 2026: "5,711 as of 2 days ago"; a passing count, so we say about 5,700. INTF's 2023 count after removing lost manuscripts was 5,541.). More catalogue numbers have been handed out over the years (at least 6,014), but some of those manuscripts are lost, destroyed or turned out to be parts of the same book, so we use the lower range.

about 141
About 141 of these are papyri, the earliest group, mostly fragments.
ConfirmedSource
about 500,000
Scholars estimate about 500,000 differences (variants) among the Greek manuscripts, not counting spelling.
ConfirmedSource
Detail

That sounds alarming, but it comes from having so many copies. Every extra copy adds its own small slips, so more copies means more variants. Most are word order, small words, or obvious copying mistakes. Peter Gurry, who made the estimate, says the number reflects how often scribes copied, not that they were unfaithful.

probably 2nd century
The earliest New Testament fragment, P52 (a few verses of John 18), is probably from the 2nd century.
ConfirmedSource
Detail

Its first editor dated it 100-150 by the handwriting. Specialists now say handwriting can only give a range, which runs into the later 2nd century or about 200. The John Rylands Library's own catalogue page says: "Recent research points to a date nearer to 200 CE, but there is as yet no convincing evidence that any earlier fragments from the New Testament survive."

4th century
The two oldest near-complete Bibles, Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, were copied in the 4th century.
ConfirmedSource
about 200 / 3rd century
Several larger papyri are commonly dated about 200 or the 3rd century: P46 (Paul's letters, INTF 'c. 200'), P66 (John, about 200) and P75 (Luke and John, Vatican says early 3rd century). Some specialists argue P66 and P75 could be 4th century.
ContestedSource

Three passages familiar from older Bibles are not in the earliest copies: John 5:4, Mark 16:9-20 and John 7:53-8:11.

Modern Bibles mark them with brackets or footnotes. Hiding nothing is the point: we know they are later because we have so many early copies to compare.

Papyrus 75 (Bodmer XIV-XV, Hanna Papyrus 1)

About half of Luke and John; includes the end of Luke and start of John on one page

Vatican Library (Hanna Papyrus 1, 'Mater Verbi'), donated 2007; formerly P. Bodmer XIV-XV

What it shows
Where the text varies, P75 and Codex Vaticanus agree roughly 90 to 94 percent of the time (Nongbri, JBL 135, 2016, summarizing earlier studies), compared with about 75 percent for Vaticanus and Sinaiticus. If P75 is early, that shows very careful copying over more than a century. If Nongbri's later date is right, the gap is smaller.
Careful
Present the agreement as strong, and the date as debated.

What changed, and what didn't

What changed

Scribes copied by hand, so they made mistakes. Most are tiny: a spelling, a word order (Greek can say "Jesus Christ" or "Christ Jesus" with the same meaning), a small word left out or repeated. Scholars estimate about 500,000 such differences, not counting spelling (Peter Gurry, 2016). The number is high because we have so many copies. A book with two copies has very few variants. A book with thousands has many.

A few changes are bigger. Three passages found in older Bibles like the King James are not in the earliest copies:

  • John 5:4, the angel stirring the water at the pool of Bethesda. Probably added to explain why people waited by the pool (v. 7).
  • Mark 16:9-20, the longer ending of Mark. The earliest complete copies, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, end at 16:8. The added ending sums up resurrection appearances also told in the other Gospels and Acts.
  • John 7:53-8:11, the woman caught in adultery. Missing from the earliest copies and placed in different spots in later ones. Many scholars think it may preserve a true early story about Jesus, but it was not originally part of John.

Modern Bibles mark these with brackets or notes. Nobody is hiding them. We know about them precisely because we have so many early copies to check against.

What didn't change

No core Christian belief rests on a disputed passage. Jesus' death and resurrection, his divinity, salvation by grace, and the Trinity are each taught in many passages whose wording is secure in the early copies. If you removed all three passages above, nothing in the creeds would change.

Even Bart Ehrman, the best-known skeptical scholar of the manuscripts, has said as much. In a Q&A printed in the 2005 paperback of Misquoting Jesus (HarperSanFrancisco, pp. 252-253), he says his view does not really conflict with Bruce Metzger's view that the essential Christian beliefs are not affected by the differences between manuscripts. To be fair to him, he adds that some differences do matter for how particular passages are read. (Paraphrased in our own words. Source seen: a transcription with photos of pp. 252-253; the book itself not yet checked, so we do not quote his exact words.)

In the Berean Standard Bible, Mark's Gospel in the earliest copies ends at 16:8: "So the women left the tomb and ran away, trembling and bewildered. And in their fear they did not say a word to anyone." (Mark 16:8, BSB)

Answer card hook (for Alfred's card 4, "The Bible was changed")

  • 30-second answer: We have thousands of early copies. Comparing them shows where scribes slipped, and modern Bibles footnote the few places where something was added. No core belief depends on those places.
  • Go deeper: Gurry 2016; Hixson and Gurry eds., Myths and Mistakes in New Testament Textual Criticism (IVP 2019); CSNTM manuscript images.

Numbers you may hear that we don’t use

  • We don't use 5,800+ (or 5,900+) Greek manuscripts

    Counts catalogue numbers, including lost, destroyed and duplicate entries.

    Say instead: About 5,500 to 5,700 surviving (about 5,711 in the April 2026 Liste).

  • We don't use 24,000 (or 25,000+) New Testament manuscripts

    Adds Latin, Syriac, Coptic and other translations using old rough estimates, not an actual count.

    Say instead: 'About 5,600 Greek copies, plus thousands more in early translations.'

  • We don't use The New Testament is 99% accurate / only 1% of variants matter

    No study produced those exact figures.

    Say instead: Most variants are spelling, word order or obvious slips; a small share both change meaning and could be original, and no core belief depends on one of them.

  • We don't use P52 dates to AD 125

    Handwriting dating cannot be that precise; the honest range runs to about 200.

    Say instead: 'The earliest fragment, probably 2nd century.'

  • We don't use A first-century fragment of Mark

    The Egypt Exploration Society published it in 2018 as P.Oxy. LXXXIII 5345 (P137) and assigned it to the late second to early third century AD. The 'first century' idea came from a quick provisional 'I/II' note on a 1980s record card.

    Say instead: Do not use.

  • We don't use Comparison tables (Homer 643 copies, Caesar 10, etc.)

    The numbers in these popular tables are out of date and often wrong, and skeptics know it.

    Say instead: Make the case from New Testament evidence itself.

  • We don't use We have the originals / nothing was ever added

    False. Three passages were added later (see the explainer).

    Say instead: Explain how we know, and why it actually builds confidence.
