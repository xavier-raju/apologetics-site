ConfirmedPool of Siloam, Jerusalem· Built no earlier than about AD 30/31, in the years Pilate governed

Pilgrimage Road (Stepped Street)

A paved street, about 600 m long and about 8 m wide, running up from the Pool of Siloam toward the Temple Mount. More than 100 coins sealed under the paving end with coins of AD 30/31. The dig runs under the Palestinian neighborhood of Silwan and is carried out with the ELAD (City of David) foundation.

What it shows
A major pilgrim route to the Temple was in use in Jerusalem during the years of Jesus' ministry, and its building date falls in the time Pontius Pilate governed Judea.
What it doesn't prove
It does not prove that Pilate himself commissioned the street, which is an inference from the coins, and some reports still say Herod or Pilate. It does not prove that Jesus walked on it.
Sources
  • Israel Antiquities Authority excavation report (Hadashot Arkheologiyot)
  • Szanton, Hagbi, Uziel, Ariel, Tel Aviv 46(2), 2019, pp. 147-166
  • Times of Israel: road opens to the public (Jan 2026)
ConfirmedPool of Siloam, Jerusalem· Second Temple period

Pool of Siloam

A large stepped pool from the Second Temple period at the south end of ancient Jerusalem. Only part of it has been uncovered so far, and no final report has been published. The site lies in Silwan and is run with the City of David foundation.

What it shows
A large public pool stood at the south end of Jerusalem in Jesus' day, at the place long identified as Siloam, which fits John 9:7.
What it doesn't prove
It does not prove the healing of the man born blind; it shows only that the place in the story is real.
Sources
  • City of David: entrance to the Siloam Pool
  • Pool of Siloam overview
ConfirmedMagdala· Early first century AD

Magdala synagogue and Magdala stone

Israel Antiquities Authority archaeologists uncovered a synagogue from the early first century at Magdala on the Sea of Galilee. Inside was a carved stone block showing a menorah, one of the earliest known in a synagogue setting.

What it shows
Galilean towns had synagogues in Jesus' lifetime, which fits the Gospel picture of him teaching in synagogues across Galilee.
What it doesn't prove
It does not prove Jesus taught in this synagogue, only that he could have. Magdala as Mary Magdalene's hometown rests on her name and tradition.
Sources
  • Israel Antiquities Authority: Magdala
  • NPR: The Magdala Stone (2015)
Contestedel-Araj (candidate for Bethsaida)· Roman and Byzantine periods

el-Araj excavations

A dig on the north shore of the Sea of Galilee has found a Roman-period settlement and a Byzantine church that tradition linked to the apostles. Finds include coins, lamps and chalkstone vessels typical of Jewish households. Excavators see el-Araj as a leading candidate for Bethsaida; et-Tell, a few kilometers north, is the rival candidate.

What it shows
A Jewish fishing settlement of the first century stood here, and by the Byzantine period Christians honored the site in connection with the apostles.
What it doesn't prove
It does not settle that el-Araj is Bethsaida, which scholars still debate, and it does not identify the house of Peter or any apostle.
Sources
  • R. S. Notley and M. Aviam, "Searching for Bethsaida: The Case for El-Araj" (BAS Library)
  • el-Araj excavation season reports
ConfirmedNazareth· First century AD

First-century house under the Sisters of Nazareth convent

A partly rock-cut courtyard house of the first century, built by Jewish residents of Nazareth. In the Byzantine period a church was built over it and it was honored as Jesus' childhood home.

What it shows
Nazareth was an inhabited Jewish village in Jesus' day, which answers the claim that the town did not exist then.
What it doesn't prove
It does not prove this was Jesus' family home. Ken Dark treats that link as possible, based on the later Byzantine tradition, but not shown.
Sources
  • K. Dark, "Early Roman-period Nazareth and the Sisters of Nazareth Convent," Antiquaries Journal 92 (2012)
  • Archaeological recording at the Sisters of Nazareth Convent, 2006 (CentAUR)
  • Ken Dark, The Sisters of Nazareth Convent, Routledge 2020, ISBN 9780367408237
  • Ken Dark, earlier paper (University of Reading, open access)
ConfirmedCaesarea Maritima· About AD 26-36

Pilate stone

A limestone block from Caesarea Maritima with a Latin dedication that names Pontius Pilate as prefect of Judea. The title is partly restored as praefectus Iudaeae. The stone came from a building honoring Tiberius (the Tiberieum), which Alföldy argued was a harbor lighthouse tower; it was later reused in the theatre.

What it shows
Pilate was a real Roman governor of Judea with the title prefect, matching the Gospels and the outside writers who name him.
What it doesn't prove
It says nothing about Jesus or the trial; it confirms the governor, not the events.
Sources
  • G. Alföldy, "Pontius Pilatus und das Tiberieum von Caesarea Maritima," Scripta Classica Israelica 18 (1999) 85-108
  • Pilate stone overview
ConfirmedHerodium· First century BC to mid-first century AD

Sealing ring inscribed "of Pilatus"

A simple copper-alloy sealing ring found at Herodium, near Bethlehem, carries a wine vessel image and a Greek inscription reading "of Pilatus." Its owner was most likely someone in Pilate's administration; the authors also raise the possibility that it was Pilate's own.

What it shows
The name Pilatus was in use in Judea in this period, and it adds a further trace of Pilate's administration.
What it doesn't prove
It does not show the ring was Pilate's own (a plain copper-alloy ring is an odd fit for a governor, and the inscription gives no title), and it says nothing about Jesus.
Sources
  • Amorai-Stark, Hershkovitz, Foerster, Kalman, Chachy, Porat, "An Inscribed Copper-Alloy Finger Ring from Herodium Depicting a Krater," Israel Exploration Journal 68(2), 2018, pp. 208-220
  • IEJ 68/2 contents with abstracts
  • Pontius Pilate overview (section on the Herodium ring)
ConfirmedJerusalem· First century AD

Caiaphas ossuary

A decorated bone box from a Jerusalem family tomb, inscribed "Joseph son of Caiaphas." It is now in the Israel Museum.

What it shows
A priestly family named Caiaphas buried its dead in Jerusalem in this era, which fits the high priest Josephus calls Joseph Caiaphas.
What it doesn't prove
Most scholars accept the link to the high priest, but the inscription itself does not say high priest; it does not touch the events of the trial.
Sources
  • Z. Greenhut, "Burial Cave of the Caiaphas Family" (BAS Library)
  • Israel Museum: Ossuary of Caiaphas exhibition page · Timed out from our box; recheck in a browser.
  • Caiaphas ossuary overview
ConfirmedJerusalem· First century AD

Heel bone of Yehohanan with nail

A man's heel bone with an iron nail still through it was found in an ossuary from a Jewish family tomb. The ossuary names him Yehohanan.

What it shows
Romans crucified people near Jerusalem in this period, and a crucified man could receive a proper Jewish burial in a family tomb, which fits the Gospel account of Jesus' burial.
What it doesn't prove
It is not connected to Jesus, and on its own it does not prove any detail of his crucifixion or burial.
Sources
  • J. Zias and E. Sekeles, "The Crucified Man from Giv'at ha-Mivtar: A Reappraisal," Israel Exploration Journal 35(1), 1985, pp. 22-27
  • Yehohanan ben Hagkol overview
ContestedCapernaum (Tell Hum)· Synagogue 4th-5th century on earlier foundations; house from the first century

Capernaum synagogue and "house of Peter"

Capernaum holds a white limestone synagogue from the late Roman period, built over older basalt foundations, and a house that early Christians turned into a meeting place and later an octagonal church.

What it shows
Capernaum was a lived-in Jewish fishing town on the lake, and Christians were honoring one of its houses from an early date.
What it doesn't prove
It does not prove the house belonged to Peter or that the visible synagogue is the one in the Gospels; the standing building is later.
Sources
  • V. Corbo, The House of Saint Peter at Capharnaum, Studium Biblicum Franciscanum (Collectio Minor 5), 1969
  • Capernaum overview
ConfirmedPool of Bethesda, Jerusalem· Second Temple period, with later Roman and Byzantine buildings

Pool of Bethesda

Digs by the Church of St Anne found twin pools with a dividing wall, which fits John 5:2's pool near the Sheep Gate with five covered colonnades. Later Roman and Byzantine buildings were built over the site.

What it shows
John's description of this Jerusalem pool reflects real local knowledge of the city before AD 70.
What it doesn't prove
It does not prove the healing of the paralyzed man.
Sources
  • Palestine Exploration Fund Quarterly Statement, July 1888 (Schick's discovery)
  • C. Schick, "Pool of Bethesda," PEQ 1888
  • Pool of Bethesda overview
ConfirmedSea of Galilee· About 1st century BC to 1st century AD

Ancient Galilee Boat

A wooden fishing boat preserved in the mud of the Sea of Galilee during a drought, now shown at the Yigal Allon Centre.

What it shows
What fishing boats on the lake looked like in the era of the Gospels.
What it doesn't prove
It is not linked to Jesus or the disciples.
Sources
  • Yigal Allon Centre: The Ancient Boat
  • Sea of Galilee Boat overview
ContestedNazareth· Probably first century BC or AD (debated)

Nazareth inscription

A Greek marble tablet with an imperial order against disturbing tombs. A 2020 study found the marble came from the island of Kos and proposed it first related to events there.

What it shows
Roman authorities treated grave robbing as a serious crime.
What it doesn't prove
It does not refer to Jesus or the empty tomb, and the 2020 study makes a Nazareth origin doubtful.
Sources
  • Harper et al., Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports 30 (2020) 102228
ConfirmedNida (Roman town at modern Frankfurt)· About AD 230-270 (approximate)

Frankfurt silver inscription

A tiny rolled silver amulet from a Roman grave at Frankfurt carries a Latin text naming Jesus Christ and echoing Philippians 2:10-11.

What it shows
Faith in Jesus had reached the Roman frontier north of the Alps by the third century.
What it doesn't prove
It is far later than the life of Jesus, so it speaks to the spread of Christianity, not to the events of the Gospels.
Sources
  • Goethe University Frankfurt release (2024)
ContestedLocation not mapped· About AD 230 per excavators (some argue later)

Megiddo mosaic ("God Jesus Christ")

A floor mosaic in an early Christian prayer hall dedicates a table to "God Jesus Christ."

What it shows
Christians were worshiping Jesus as God in an organized setting in the region by the third or early fourth century.
What it doesn't prove
The exact date is debated, and it says nothing direct about first-century events.
Sources
  • Israel Antiquities Authority excavation report, Legio, Kefar 'Otnay (2006)
  • Israel Antiquities Authority: Megiddo Prison excavation summary
  • Y. Tepper and L. Di Segni, A Christian Prayer Hall of the Third Century CE at Kefar 'Othnay (Legio), IAA 2006, ISBN 9654061937
  • Megiddo church overview
