Suffering and death

The Suffering Servant

direct predictionContested

Old Testament · Isaiah 52:13-53:12

13 Behold, My Servant will prosper; He will be raised and lifted up and highly exalted.

14 Just as many were appalled at Him—His appearance was disfigured beyond that of any man, and His form was marred beyond human likeness—

15 so He will sprinkle many nations. Kings will shut their mouths because of Him. For they will see what they have not been told, and they will understand what they have not heard.

1 Who has believed our message? And to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?

2 He grew up before Him like a tender shoot, and like a root out of dry ground. He had no stately form or majesty to attract us, no beauty that we should desire Him.

3 He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows, acquainted with grief. Like one from whom men hide their faces, He was despised, and we esteemed Him not.

4 Surely He took up our infirmities and carried our sorrows; yet we considered Him stricken, struck down by God, and afflicted.

5 But He was pierced for our transgressions, He was crushed for our iniquities; the punishment that brought us peace was upon Him, and by His stripes we are healed.

6 We all like sheep have gone astray, each one has turned to his own way; and the LORD has laid upon Him the iniquity of us all.

7 He was oppressed and afflicted, yet He did not open His mouth. He was led like a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is silent, so He did not open His mouth.

8 By oppression and judgment He was taken away, and who can recount His descendants? For He was cut off from the land of the living; He was stricken for the transgression of My people.

9 He was assigned a grave with the wicked, and with a rich man in His death, although He had done no violence, nor was any deceit in His mouth.

10 Yet it was the LORD’s will to crush Him and to cause Him to suffer; and when His soul is made a guilt offering, He will see His offspring, He will prolong His days, and the good pleasure of the LORD will prosper in His hand.

11 After the anguish of His soul, He will see the light of life and be satisfied. By His knowledge My righteous Servant will justify many, and He will bear their iniquities.

12 Therefore I will allot Him a portion with the great, and He will divide the spoils with the strong, because He has poured out His life unto death, and He was numbered with the transgressors. Yet He bore the sin of many and made intercession for the transgressors.

New Testament

33 In His humiliation He was deprived of justice. Who can recount His descendants? For His life was removed from the earth.”

34 “Tell me,” said the eunuch, “who is the prophet talking about, himself or someone else?”

35 Then Philip began with this very Scripture and told him the good news about Jesus.

22 “He committed no sin, and no deceit was found in His mouth.”

23 When they heaped abuse on Him, He did not retaliate; when He suffered, He made no threats, but entrusted Himself to Him who judges justly.

24 He Himself bore our sins in His body on the tree, so that we might die to sin and live to righteousness. “By His stripes you are healed.”

25 For “you were like sheep going astray,” but now you have returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls.

17 This was to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah: “He took up our infirmities and carried our diseases.”

37 For I tell you that this Scripture must be fulfilled in Me: ‘And He was numbered with the transgressors.’ For what is written about Me is reaching its fulfillment.”

16 But not all of them welcomed the good news. For Isaiah says, “Lord, who has believed our message?”

57 When it was evening, there came a rich man from Arimathea named Joseph, who himself was a disciple of Jesus.

58 He went to Pilate to ask for the body of Jesus, and Pilate ordered that it be given to him.

59 So Joseph took the body, wrapped it in a clean linen cloth,

60 and placed it in his own new tomb that he had cut into the rock. Then he rolled a great stone across the entrance to the tomb and went away.

Two readings, fairly stated

Because the details fit only Jesus. The servant is innocent, yet he is rejected and struck down 'for the transgression of My people', so he is distinct from the people he suffers for. The LORD lays on him 'the iniquity of us all' and makes his soul 'a guilt offering'. He is counted among sinners, dies, and is buried with a rich man, as Jesus was laid in the new tomb of Joseph of Arimathea, 'a rich man' (Matthew 27:57-60). Then 'He will see His offspring, He will prolong His days'. That is a substitute who dies and then lives again. Philip uses the passage to tell the Ethiopian official about Jesus (Acts 8), Peter applies it to Christ's sinless death (1 Peter 2), and Jesus says it is fulfilled in him (Luke 22:37). The earliest Christian writers outside the New Testament, Clement of Rome and Justin Martyr, read it the same way.

Jewish interpretation of this passage has a long history with more than one view. Many early Jewish readings apply it to the Messiah, though Origen (about AD 248) already met Jews who read it of the nation. The Aramaic Targum Jonathan reads Isaiah 52:13 as 'my servant Messiah', though it applies the sufferings of chapter 53 mostly to others: the nation, the Temple and the wicked. The Talmud (Sanhedrin 98b) names the Messiah 'the leprous one' or 'the sick one' from Isaiah 53:4, and the later Zohar (about the 13th century) pictures the Messiah taking on Israel's sicknesses and pains. From Rashi (11th century) on, followed by Ibn Ezra and the Kimchis, the reading of the servant as the people of Israel became the majority Jewish view. Israel is called God's servant elsewhere in Isaiah (41:8; 49:3), and on this view the speakers in chapter 53 are the nations, who come to see that Israel's suffering in exile was not what they assumed. Some medieval Jewish teachers, such as Moshe Kohen ibn Crispin, still held to the Messiah reading. Jewish teachers today often point to details they say do not fit Jesus, such as 'he will see offspring and prolong his days.'

Note. The heart of the question is whether the servant is one person or the nation. The text answers it: in 53:8 the servant suffers 'for the transgression of My people', so he is not the people. Isaiah 49:3 calls the servant 'Israel', but in 49:5-6 he restores Israel, so Christians see him as the one true Israel, the faithful Israelite who embodies the nation and brings it back. And while everyone in Isaiah confesses sin, the prophet included (6:5; 53:6), the servant alone is sinless (53:9). An innocent individual who dies for others and then lives again points to Jesus.
