Suffering and death

My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?

typologicalContested

Old Testament · Psalm 22:1,7-8,14-18

1 For the choirmaster. To the tune of “The Doe of the Dawn.” A Psalm of David. My God, my God, why have You forsaken me? Why are You so far from saving me, so far from my words of groaning?

7 All who see me mock me; they sneer and shake their heads:

8 “He trusts in the LORD, let the LORD deliver him; let the LORD rescue him, since He delights in him.”

14 I am poured out like water, and all my bones are disjointed. My heart is like wax; it melts away within me.

15 My strength is dried up like a potsherd, and my tongue sticks to the roof of my mouth. You lay me in the dust of death.

16 For dogs surround me; a band of evil men encircles me; they have pierced my hands and feet.

17 I can count all my bones; they stare and gloat over me.

18 They divide my garments among them and cast lots for my clothing.

New Testament

35 When they had crucified Him, they divided up His garments by casting lots.

39 And those who passed by heaped abuse on Him, shaking their heads

40 and saying, “You who are going to destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days, save Yourself! If You are the Son of God, come down from the cross!”

41 In the same way, the chief priests, scribes, and elders mocked Him, saying,

42 “He saved others, but He cannot save Himself. He is the King of Israel! Let Him come down now from the cross, and we will believe in Him.

43 He trusts in God. Let God deliver Him now if He wants Him. For He said, ‘I am the Son of God.’”

46 About the ninth hour Jesus cried out in a loud voice, “Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?” which means, “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?”

23 When the soldiers had crucified Jesus, they divided His garments into four parts, one for each soldier, with the tunic remaining. It was seamless, woven in one piece from top to bottom.

24 So they said to one another, “Let us not tear it. Instead, let us cast lots to see who will get it.” This was to fulfill the Scripture: “They divided My garments among them, and cast lots for My clothing.” So that is what the soldiers did.

12 He says: “I will proclaim Your name to My brothers; I will sing Your praises in the assembly.”

Two readings, fairly stated

Jesus prays the first line of this psalm from the cross. The Gospels then echo it in the mocking crowd, the shaking of heads, and the soldiers dividing his clothes by lot. The psalm moves from abandonment to rescue and worldwide praise (22:22-31), and Christians read it as the shape of the cross and resurrection.

Jewish readers take the psalm as the prayer of David, or of the righteous sufferer or the people of Israel in distress, who are rescued by God. In verse 16 (Hebrew 22:17) the standard Hebrew text reads ka'ari, 'like a lion [at] my hands and feet', rather than 'they pierced', and Jewish teachers often point this out.

Note. The 'pierced' wording is a real textual question. The Greek Septuagint, made before Christianity, reads 'they dug', and a Dead Sea Scrolls-era fragment from Nahal Hever is often cited for a verb here. Our team has not yet confirmed which plate shows this, so we treat it as Contested.
