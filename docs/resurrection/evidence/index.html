The five accounts side by side

Five early texts tell what happened. Each gives something and leaves something out. Pick one to see what it adds and where it stops. The differences are what you would expect from independent tellings, not a single copied script.

FramingLetter c. AD 53-55; the creed it quotes is earlier

1 Corinthians 15:3-8

What it gives
Death, burial, raising on the third day, and a list of appearances to named people and groups.

Its limit
No women, no named tomb and no scene of the discovery.

Mark 16:1-8

Its limit

William Lane Craig's four facts

Craig sums up the case in four facts he says most scholars who write on the subject accept, and asks what best explains them. Each links back to the matching fact on the hub.

Framing

1. Joseph of Arimathea buried him

30-second version

Jesus was laid in a tomb by a known member of the council that condemned him, which made the grave's location public knowledge.

The matching fact on the hub→

Go deeper

Craig argues that a Christian invention would be unlikely to credit a member of the hostile council with an honourable burial, and that the burial sits in the very early tradition Paul quotes ("he was buried", 1 Cor 15:4). The Givat ha-Mivtar tomb below shows that a crucified man could end up in a family tomb.

Sources

  • William Lane Craig, "The Resurrection of Jesus," Reasonable Faith (popular writings)
  • Vassilios Tzaferis, "A Tomb in Jerusalem Reveals the History of Crucifixion and Roman Crucifixion Methods," Biblical Archaeology Society
Contested

2. Women found the tomb empty

30-second version

On the Sunday a group of women found the tomb empty. Craig counts this among facts most scholars grant; Habermas's count is about 75%.

The matching fact on the hub→

Go deeper

Craig points out that a made-up story would hardly pick women as the first witnesses in a culture that gave their testimony little weight. The earliest known counter-claim, that the disciples stole the body (Matt 28:11-15, and Justin about AD 160), assumes the tomb was empty. On this site the empty tomb stays Contested, because Habermas's figure is a majority, not near-unanimity.

Sources

  • William Lane Craig, "The Resurrection of Jesus," Reasonable Faith (popular writings)
  • Gary Habermas, "Resurrection Research from 1975 to the Present," Journal for the Study of the Historical Jesus 3.2 (2005)
  • Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 108, New Advent
Confirmed

3. People saw him alive afterwards

30-second version

Individuals and groups, friends and former opponents, reported seeing Jesus alive after his death.

The matching fact on the hub→

Go deeper

Paul's list in 1 Cor 15:5-8 names Peter, the Twelve, a group of more than 500, James, all the apostles and Paul himself. Craig, like Habermas, counts the appearances as granted by most scholars, while they argue over what the people saw.

Sources

  • William Lane Craig, "The Resurrection of Jesus," Reasonable Faith (popular writings)
  • Gary Habermas, "The Minimal Facts Approach to the Resurrection of Jesus," Southeastern Theological Review 3/1 (2012)
Framing

4. The disciples suddenly believed he was raised

30-second version

The first followers came to believe, suddenly and sincerely, that God had raised Jesus, against everything they had expected.

The matching fact on the hub→

Go deeper

Craig's point is that the Jews of the time had no belief in a dying, let alone rising, Messiah, and expected the dead to rise together at the end of the world, not one man in the middle of history. A crucified Messiah looked like a failed one. Something persuaded the disciples otherwise.

Sources

  • William Lane Craig, "The Resurrection of Jesus," Reasonable Faith (popular writings)

Habermas's count of scholars

Where "most scholars agree" comes from, and what it does and does not mean.

Confirmed

How big is the count?

30-second version

Habermas says his bibliography on Jesus' death and resurrection reached about 3,400 publications in English, French and German.

Go deeper

This is a reading list, not an opinion poll. Habermas says he catalogued scholarly writing from 1975 onward, across the whole spectrum from sceptical to conservative, and sorted the positions each writer takes. An earlier report in 2005 put the figure at more than 1,400 publications. When you see 'most scholars agree' on this site, this is the kind of evidence behind it.

Sources

  • Gary Habermas, "The Minimal Facts Approach to the Resurrection of Jesus," Southeastern Theological Review 3/1 (2012)
  • Gary Habermas, "Resurrection Research from 1975 to the Present," Journal for the Study of the Historical Jesus 3.2 (2005)
Confirmed

How high is the bar?

30-second version

To make his short list, a fact must be granted by nearly everyone. In his words, think of "a ninety-something percentile head-count."

Go deeper

Habermas separates facts that almost all specialists grant from facts that only a majority grant. Only the first group goes on his short list. He also notes that his count leans sceptical rather than conservative, because it includes radical writers who are not specialists. So the short list is not stacked in favour of believers.

Sources

  • Gary Habermas, "The Minimal Facts Approach to the Resurrection of Jesus," Southeastern Theological Review 3/1 (2012)
Confirmed

The one percentage he published

30-second version

On the empty tomb, about 75% of the scholars he counted favour one or more arguments for it; about 25% favour one or more against.

Go deeper

This is the only percentage we print, because it is the only one Habermas published. It is a count of arguments in the literature, not a formal poll, and it is why this site treats the empty tomb as strong but Contested rather than near-unanimous.

Sources

  • Gary Habermas, "Resurrection Research from 1975 to the Present," Journal for the Study of the Historical Jesus 3.2 (2005)

A crucified man's tomb

ConfirmedArchaeology

A crucified man in a family tomb

30-second version

In 1968 a Jerusalem tomb at Giv'at ha-Mivtar held the bones of a crucified man, Yehohanan, with an iron nail still through his heel bone.

Go deeper

Vassilios Tzaferis excavated the tomb northeast of Jerusalem. The bones were in an ossuary (a bone box) inscribed with the man's name. It is the first physical evidence of crucifixion from antiquity. For the resurrection case its main point is narrower: a man executed by crucifixion could still be given a proper family burial, which fits the Gospels' account of Joseph's tomb. It does not prove anything about Jesus' own tomb.

Sources

  • Vassilios Tzaferis, "A Tomb in Jerusalem Reveals the History of Crucifixion and Roman Crucifixion Methods," Biblical Archaeology Society
  • Matthew Maslen and Piers Mitchell, "Medical theories on the cause of death in crucifixion," Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine (2006)

The first writers after the apostles

Public-domain translations, linked so you can read every chapter yourself.

ConfirmedRome, c. AD 95-97 (Contested range)

1 Clement

30-second version

A letter from the church at Rome says the apostles went out preaching "fully assured by the resurrection of our Lord Jesus Christ."

Go deeper

Chapter 42 describes the apostles receiving their orders and going out to preach because they were convinced of the resurrection. It shows that within about two generations the resurrection was the reason given for the whole mission.

Sources

  • 1 Clement 42 (Roberts-Donaldson translation), New Advent
Confirmedc. AD 110 (Contested range)

Ignatius of Antioch

30-second version

Ignatius insists Jesus had real flesh after rising: "I know that after His resurrection also He was still possessed of flesh."

Go deeper

In his letter to the Smyrnaeans, chapter 3, Ignatius argues against people who said Jesus only seemed to have a body. He recalls Jesus telling Peter's group to touch him and see he was not a bodiless spirit (compare Luke 24:39). A bodily resurrection, not a vision, was the claim from early on.

Sources

  • Ignatius, Letter to the Smyrnaeans 3, New Advent
ConfirmedRome, c. AD 155 (Contested range)

Justin Martyr, First Apology

30-second version

Justin explains that Christians meet on Sunday because it is the day Jesus rose from the dead.

Go deeper

In chapter 67 Justin describes Sunday worship to the emperor and gives two reasons: God began creation on the first day, and Jesus rose on that same day. The weekly habit itself is a trace of the Easter claim.

Sources

  • Justin Martyr, First Apology 67, New Advent
Confirmedc. AD 160 (Contested range)

Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho

30-second version

Justin reports that opponents were spreading the story that "his disciples stole him by night from the tomb."

Go deeper

In chapter 108 Justin says men were sent out to say that the disciples stole the body. That counter-story, also found in Matthew 28:11-15, concedes the key point: the tomb was empty. The argument was over how it became empty.

Sources

  • Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 108, New Advent

Wright and Allison

Two of the most detailed recent studies, from scholars who reach different levels of confidence.

FramingNew Testament scholar

N. T. Wright

30-second version

Wright argues that early Christianity cannot be explained unless the tomb was empty and people met Jesus alive afterwards.

Go deeper

In his 1998 essay he treats 1 Corinthians 15 as the earliest written testimony, carrying tradition from the very first days. In his 2005 reply to four critics he restates his view: the best historical explanation is an empty tomb plus sightings of Jesus bodily alive, and the best explanation of those two is that Jesus was raised. He answers critics who say visions alone could have produced belief in an empty tomb.

Sources

  • N. T. Wright, "Early Traditions and the Origins of Christianity," Sewanee Theological Review 41.2 (1998)
  • N. T. Wright, "Resurrecting Old Arguments: Responding to Four Essays," Journal for the Study of the Historical Jesus 3.2 (2005)
ContestedNew Testament scholar

Dale C. Allison Jr.

30-second version

Allison believes Jesus rose, but writes that the historical evidence is "not so good as to make disbelief unreasonable, and it is not so bad as to make faith untenable."

Go deeper

His 2021 book is one of the most thorough recent studies, with long treatments of visions, apparitions and group experiences. We have not read the book itself; the quote above is as given in Andrew Loke's 2022 review essay, which engages Allison's discussion of hallucination theory and mass hysteria and argues that the strongest sceptical hypothesis Allison raises does not hold up. We include Allison because he is careful and fair, not because he agrees with this site.

Sources

  • Andrew Loke, "The Resurrection of Jesus: An Engagement with Dale Allison: A Review Essay" (2022), abstract
  • Dale C. Allison Jr., The Resurrection of Jesus: Apologetics, Polemics, History (T&T Clark, 2021), publisher page

Strobel's interview room

Lee Strobel was legal editor of the Chicago Tribune and an atheist. After almost two years looking into the evidence he became a Christian in 1981. The Case for Christ (1998) is built as a set of interviews; Part 3 is about the resurrection.
