ConfirmedChristian (critical scholar)Link only

Dale C. Allison Jr.

New Testament scholar (Princeton Theological Seminary). He is a Christian, and a cautious critical scholar on what history alone can prove.

Allison thinks the tomb was probably empty and that Mary Magdalene and Peter really believed they saw Jesus. He says history alone cannot prove God raised him.

Accepts

  • the arguments in favor of an empty tomb are better than those against it

    Dale C. Allison Jr. (interviewed by Pete Enns and Jared Byas), Episode 206: Dale C. Allison, Approaching the Resurrection of Jesus as a Historian (The Bible for Normal People, published transcript), 2022 · transcript, first answer on his conclusions

  • there were people who believed that they saw Jesus after He was dead after he had been crucified

    Dale C. Allison Jr. (interviewed by Pete Enns and Jared Byas), Episode 206: Dale C. Allison, Approaching the Resurrection of Jesus as a Historian (The Bible for Normal People, published transcript), 2022 · transcript, same answer (Mary Magdalene and Peter named)

Denies

  • Those three things don't add up to the Christian conclusion

    Dale C. Allison Jr. (interviewed by Pete Enns and Jared Byas), Episode 206: Dale C. Allison, Approaching the Resurrection of Jesus as a Historian (The Bible for Normal People, published transcript), 2022 · transcript, discussion of tomb, visions and expectations

  • Paul's statement there, I think is a dead end.

    Dale C. Allison Jr. (interviewed by Pete Enns and Jared Byas), Episode 206: Dale C. Allison, Approaching the Resurrection of Jesus as a Historian (The Bible for Normal People, published transcript), 2022 · transcript, discussion of 1 Cor 15:6

Go deeper

Allison is one of the most careful living specialists on the resurrection. In a 2022 interview he said the case for an empty tomb is stronger than the case against it, though not certain, and that early followers, probably first Mary Magdalene and then Peter, believed they saw Jesus after his death. He also holds that these facts do not by themselves force the Christian conclusion, and he finds Paul's brief mention of the 500 too thin to test. He is useful because he grants key facts without being an apologist.

ConfirmedScepticLink only

Bart D. Ehrman

New Testament scholar at UNC Chapel Hill; agnostic; former evangelical.

Ehrman grants the crucifixion and that followers, Paul included, had visions of Jesus. He doubts the burial and the empty tomb and says a miracle is never the historian's best answer.

Accepts

  • They had believed Jesus was the Messiah, but then he got crucified

    William Lane Craig and Bart D. Ehrman, Is There Historical Evidence for the Resurrection of Jesus? The Craig-Ehrman Debate (transcript), 2006 · Concluding statement, Dr. Ehrman

  • Believers who knew he had been raised from the dead started having visions of him.

    William Lane Craig and Bart D. Ehrman, Is There Historical Evidence for the Resurrection of Jesus? The Craig-Ehrman Debate (transcript), 2006 · Concluding statement, Dr. Ehrman

  • Some of them were actual visions like Paul

    William Lane Craig and Bart D. Ehrman, Is There Historical Evidence for the Resurrection of Jesus? The Craig-Ehrman Debate (transcript), 2006 · Concluding statement, Dr. Ehrman

Denies

  • We don't know if Jesus was buried by Joseph of Arimathea.

    William Lane Craig and Bart D. Ehrman, Is There Historical Evidence for the Resurrection of Jesus? The Craig-Ehrman Debate (transcript), 2006 · First rebuttal, Dr. Ehrman

  • We don't know if his tomb was empty three days later.

    William Lane Craig and Bart D. Ehrman, Is There Historical Evidence for the Resurrection of Jesus? The Craig-Ehrman Debate (transcript), 2006 · First rebuttal, Dr. Ehrman

  • Bill's explanation is a theological explanation.

    William Lane Craig and Bart D. Ehrman, Is There Historical Evidence for the Resurrection of Jesus? The Craig-Ehrman Debate (transcript), 2006 · Opening statement, Dr. Ehrman

Go deeper

In the 2006 Holy Cross debate Ehrman granted that Jesus was crucified and that believers, including Paul, had what he called visions of Jesus. He argued that we cannot know whether Joseph of Arimathea buried Jesus or whether the tomb was empty, and that a miracle can never be the most probable historical explanation. Craig cited Ehrman's earlier Teaching Company lectures as granting the burial and empty tomb; Ehrman said in the debate that he does not agree with that reading, so we show only what he said in the debate.

ConfirmedScepticLink only

Gerd Lüdemann

German New Testament scholar (Göttingen, 1946 to 2021); atheist; proposed the grief-vision theory.

Lüdemann agrees Peter had a real experience of seeing Jesus. He calls it a grief vision and says the body stayed dead.

Accepts

  • Easter began with a visionary experience that happened to Peter.

    Gerd Lüdemann, A New Explanation of the Resurrection of Jesus: The Result of Mourning (Bible and Interpretation), 2012 · lead paragraph

  • Jesus' body decayed or was eaten by vultures, as was the case with most crucified persons.

    Gerd Lüdemann, A New Explanation of the Resurrection of Jesus: The Result of Mourning (Bible and Interpretation), 2012 · lead paragraph

Denies

  • Jesus' body decayed or was eaten by vultures, as was the case with most crucified persons.

    Gerd Lüdemann, A New Explanation of the Resurrection of Jesus: The Result of Mourning (Bible and Interpretation), 2012 · lead paragraph

  • his corpse was not resuscitated and he did not return to life

    Gerd Lüdemann, A New Explanation of the Resurrection of Jesus: The Result of Mourning (Bible and Interpretation), 2012 · lead paragraph

Go deeper

Lüdemann is the leading voice for the hallucination or vision theory. In a 2012 article he says Easter began with Peter's visionary experience, which he explains psychologically as guilt and grief after Peter's denial. He holds that the body decayed or was eaten, so the tomb was not found empty. The persuasive point for us: even the main skeptical theory starts from real experiences by Peter.

ConfirmedScepticLink only

John Dominic Crossan

Irish-American scholar (DePaul University), co-founder of the Jesus Seminar; reads the resurrection stories as metaphor.

Crossan treats the crucifixion as hard historical fact. He thinks Mark invented the empty tomb as a way to end his Gospel.

Accepts

  • the brute historical fact that Jesus would have died in agony on the cross

    John Dominic Crossan (interview), PBS Frontline, From Jesus to Christ: Jesus' Many Faces, Arrest and Execution, 1998 · Crossan section, on the last words of Jesus

Denies

  • Mark creates the empty tomb, as far as I can see, as his way of ending the story.

    PBS Frontline (program transcript, Crossan speaking), From Jesus to Christ: The First Christians, Part Two transcript, 1998 · Part Two, Gospel of Mark segment

Debate: October 1994, Moody Memorial Church (Illinois).

  • Reasonable Faith, Reasonable Faith Press Kit (Exceptional Debates list) · Exceptional Debates page

Go deeper

Crossan is the best-known Jesus Seminar scholar. In PBS Frontline's 1998 series he calls Jesus' death on the cross a brute historical fact, while arguing that the empty tomb is Mark's literary creation and that resurrection language is best read as metaphor. Craig debated him in October 1994 at Moody Memorial Church (Illinois) (Reasonable Faith press kit), moderated by William F. Buckley Jr. The debate is printed in Will the Real Jesus Please Stand Up? (ed. Paul Copan).

ConfirmedScepticLink only

Richard Carrier

Independent historian (PhD, Columbia, ancient history); atheist; argues Jesus probably did not exist.

Carrier doubts Jesus existed at all; by his own estimate the chance he existed is at most about 33%. Even so, in his resurrection essay he grants that early believers really had experiences, which he calls dreams or hallucinations.

Accepts

  • I think it is most likely by far that the original belief was derived from dreams, hallucinations, or "inspired" readings of scripture

    Richard Carrier, Why I Don't Buy the Resurrection Story (6th ed., 2006), 2006 · introduction

Denies

  • some evidence suggests the original conception of the resurrection of Jesus was spiritual in nature and did not involve his flesh

    Richard Carrier, Why I Don't Buy the Resurrection Story (6th ed., 2006), 2006 · introduction, reason 3

  • leaving us with almost a 33% chance Jesus existed

    Richard Carrier, Doing the Math: Historicity of Jesus Edition (Richard Carrier Blogs), 2021 · concluding section (his upper bound)

Go deeper

Carrier's position is narrow. He thinks the chance that Jesus existed is at most about 33%, so he does not grant the crucifixion or burial as facts. In his 2006 essay on the resurrection, written before his main historicity work, he grants only that the first Christians sincerely held a resurrection belief, and he explains it as arising from dreams, hallucinations or inspired readings of scripture. He argues the earliest belief was in a spiritual, not fleshly, resurrection, which removes the need for an empty tomb. We should not say Carrier 'concedes the four facts'.

ConfirmedScholarLink only

N. T. Wright

Anglican bishop and New Testament historian (Oxford, St Andrews); author of The Resurrection of the Son of God (2003).

Wright argues the early church's belief in a bodily resurrection only makes sense if something happened to Jesus' body.

Accepts

  • there is in fact no other solution to the historical problem than to conclude that something remarkable had happened to the body of Jesus

    N. T. Wright, Christian Origins and the Resurrection of Jesus: The Resurrection of Jesus as a Historical Problem (Sewanee Theological Review 41.2, 1998; reproduced on NTWrightPage), 1998 · first lecture, final paragraphs

  • they really believed Jesus had been bodily raised from the dead

    N. T. Wright, Christian Origins and the Resurrection of Jesus: The Resurrection of Jesus as a Historical Problem (Sewanee Theological Review 41.2, 1998; reproduced on NTWrightPage), 1998 · first lecture, section on the resurrection movement

Go deeper

Wright approaches the question as a historian of Second Temple Judaism. Jews who believed in resurrection expected it for everyone at the end of time, not for one man in the middle of history. He argues the only explanation for the first Christians' behaviour, stories and theology is that they really believed Jesus had been bodily raised, and that history offers no other solution than that something remarkable happened to his body.

FramingScholarLink only

C. D. Elledge

Scholar of early Judaism and the Dead Sea Scrolls at Gustavus Adolphus College; author of Resurrection of the Dead in Early Judaism.

Elledge shows that by Jesus' day many Jews expected a literal raising of the dead by God, while others rejected the idea.

Accepts

  • God would literally bring life to the dead.

    C. D. Elledge, Resurrection in Early Judaism; Shaul Shaked · opening section

  • Resurrection was also opposed or ignored by a significant proportion of the Jewish populace.

    C. D. Elledge, Resurrection in Early Judaism; Shaul Shaked · opening summary

Go deeper

Elledge does not argue about Jesus. He supplies context: by the Hellenistic era many Jews had come to expect that God would literally bring the dead back to life, while some writings preferred the immortality of the soul and many Jews opposed or ignored resurrection. This helps explain what the first Christians meant by 'raised', and why claiming it of one man before the end was unusual.

ConfirmedEarly Christian writerPublic domain

Justin Martyr

Christian philosopher from Samaria, writing in Rome about AD 150 to 160; martyred about AD 165.

Justin, writing around AD 150, reports that Jewish opponents said the disciples stole the body. That counter-claim assumes the tomb was empty.

Accepts

  • after He was crucified, even all His acquaintances forsook Him, having denied Him

    Justin Martyr, First Apology Memoirs of apostles in worship · First Apology ch. 50

  • on the day after that of Saturn, which is the day of the Sun, having appeared to His apostles and disciples

    Justin Martyr, First Apology Memoirs of apostles in worship · First Apology ch. 67

  • his disciples stole him by night from the tomb

    Justin Martyr (ANF vol. 1 translation, via New Advent), Dialogue with Trypho, chapters 101-108 page, 160 · Dialogue with Trypho ch. 108 (reporting the Jewish counter-claim)

Go deeper

Justin is a second-century Christian, not an eyewitness. He matters for two reasons. He records the Jewish polemic that the disciples stole the body by night, which concedes an empty tomb (compare Matthew 28:11-15). He also shows that Sunday worship was already tied to the resurrection appearances by the mid second century.

ContestedEarly Christian writerLink only

Gospel of Peter

A second-century Gospel fragment (Akhmim manuscript) with a dramatic tomb scene; a late source, not an independent eyewitness.

A text in use by about AD 200 that retells the burial and empty tomb with legendary additions, like a talking cross. It shows how later writers embellished, which makes the plain Gospel accounts look early by contrast.

Accepts

  • he took the Lord, and washed him, and rolled him in a linen cloth, and brought him into his own tomb

    Gospel of Peter (Akhmim fragment; English text on Early Christian Writings, translator not named on page), The Gospel According to Peter · section 6

  • they went and found the tomb opened

    Gospel of Peter (Akhmim fragment; English text on Early Christian Writings, translator not named on page), The Gospel According to Peter · section 13

Contested Dating: Serapion's rejection, recorded by Eusebius (HE 6.12), shows it was in use by about AD 200. Paul Foster prefers perhaps the second half of the 2nd century, which he calls ultimately unprovable (Foster 2010, pp. 172, 174, as quoted in Smith 2012, p. 647).

  • Daniel L. Smith, Review of Paul Foster, The Gospel of Peter, Journal of Early Christian Studies 20.4 (Winter 2012), 2012 · p. 647 (excerpt opened by TARS, 29 Oct 2026)
  • Eusebius (NPNF2 vol. 1 translation, via New Advent), Church History, Book VI, chapter 12 (Serapion on the Gospel of Peter), 325 · Book VI, chapter 12
Go deeper

The Gospel of Peter survives mainly in one later manuscript found at Akhmim in Egypt. Bishop Serapion of Antioch (about AD 200) discussed and rejected a 'Gospel of Peter' used at Rhossus, as Eusebius records. Its date is disputed. Serapion's rejection sets the latest date at about AD 200, and some scholars (Crossan, Koester, Cameron) argue it preserves an early independent passion source. Its tomb scene has two giant figures leading a third out, followed by a speaking cross. We treat it as a late source showing how the story was embellished, not as an independent witness.

ConfirmedEarly Christian writerPublic domain

Ignatius of Antioch

Bishop of Antioch who wrote seven letters on his way to martyrdom in Rome, about AD 110.

Ignatius, writing around AD 110, insists Jesus was truly crucified, truly died and was raised in the flesh.

Accepts

  • He was truly crucified, and [truly] died

    Ignatius of Antioch (ANF vol. 1 translation, via New Advent), Epistle to the Trallians, 110 · Trallians ch. 9

  • I know that after His resurrection also He was still possessed of flesh

    Ignatius, Letter to the Smyrnaeans Insistence on real birth · Smyrnaeans ch. 3

Go deeper

Ignatius wrote within living memory of the apostles' generation. Against people who said Jesus only seemed to suffer, he stresses that Jesus was truly crucified under Pontius Pilate, truly died and was truly raised, and that after his resurrection he still had flesh and ate and drank with his disciples. This shows the bodily resurrection was the mainstream belief by the early second century.

ConfirmedEarly Christian writerPublic domain

1 Clement

A letter from the church in Rome to the church in Corinth, traditionally by Clement, usually dated about AD 96.

Written from Rome around AD 96, 1 Clement says the apostles went out preaching because they were fully assured by the resurrection, and that Peter and Paul suffered for it.

Accepts

  • being fully assured by the resurrection of our Lord Jesus Christ

    First Clement, English translation Early Roman church letter; Jesus traditions · ch. 42

  • Peter, through unrighteous envy, endured not one or two, but numerous labours; and when he had at length suffered martyrdom

    First Clement, English translation Early Roman church letter; Jesus traditions · ch. 5

Go deeper

1 Clement is one of the earliest Christian writings outside the New Testament. It says the apostles went out preaching once they were fully assured by Jesus' resurrection, and it refers to the deaths of Peter and Paul in the recent past. It supports the claim that the apostles' conviction was real and costly.
